## 2. The problem: iodine that is produced but never collected

Japan is the world's second-largest iodine producer. The iodine comes from natural-gas brine pumped in Chiba Prefecture and is recovered by two commercial methods, blow-out and ion exchange, which work at raw-brine concentrations (tens of ppm to about 100 ppm) and become uneconomic below that.

From published operating data for a representative plant (35 ppm brine, 60,000 t/day, 1,400 kg/day of iodine), we estimate that only about two-thirds of the iodine entering the plant is recovered. The rest, roughly 700 kg/day at that plant and, by our estimate, **700–1,000 t/yr nationwide**, leaves with the discharged brine at 7–12 ppm. One major producer has told us its discharge carries about 10 ppm, consistent with our estimate. (The national figure is being cross-checked with the Society of Iodine Science; until then it is our estimate.)

Producers cannot pump more brine: pumping is capped by ground-subsidence agreements with local governments. **Recovering iodine from their own discharge is therefore one of the few ways left for a Japanese producer to increase output.** That is what we sell: more iodine from the same brine, with the existing process untouched.

**Where iodine goes, and why demand is not the constraint.** By volume, iodine is used in X-ray contrast media, pharmaceuticals, LCD polarizing film, iodophor disinfectants, animal feed and fluorochemicals, in that order (USGS); spot prices averaged around USD 70/kg in 2024–2025. Two newer uses add a supply-security dimension. Japanese industrial policy names iodine a principal raw material for perovskite solar cells, which Japan intends to manufacture domestically at scale. In space, iodine is displacing xenon as an electric-propulsion propellant: the first in-orbit demonstration of an iodine thruster was published in *Nature* in 2021, and iodine stores as an unpressurised solid at roughly three times xenon's density, which simplifies small-satellite systems. Xenon output is concentrated (Russia and Ukraine, about a quarter to a third of world supply) while third-party projections put more than 20,000 satellites in orbit within a decade, most requiring electric propulsion. **Propulsion volumes are small today against a roughly 34,000 t/yr iodine market, and we put none of this in our financials.** The point is directional: two industries that matter to both governments now draw on the same constrained primary supply, and Japan is the second-largest source of it.

## 3. Technology and evidence

### 3.1 How it works

The core material is a temperature-responsive polymer (the "PhaseShift agent") from the Nishimura laboratory, Doshisha University. In water it complexes with iodine and, on a modest temperature change, phase-separates into a concentrated iodine-rich phase; a further temperature swing releases the iodine. Desorption is therefore **designed** to need no reducing agents or solvents, and the heat source is **designed** to be low-grade plant waste heat (one producer has disclosed a steam-drain stream at 80–90 °C). Only the concentrated phase, about 1–3% of the flow, is heated; we estimate about 10.5 kW per unit. Whether waste heat alone suffices depends on heat-exchanger efficiency and is not yet demonstrated.

Installation is a **side-stream retrofit**: 3.5–6.8% of the discharge is diverted through a fixed-bed cartridge unit and returned; the main line never stops. This followed producer feedback that full-flow insertion was not feasible.

### 3.2 What has been measured (laboratory, batch, as of August 2026)

| System | Conventional polymer (PVP) | PhaseShift agent | Note |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Iodine in hexane | 73.9% | 91.4% | capture |
| Pure water, 7 ppm (two conditions) | — | 96.8% / 97.2% | capture, UV-vis 351 nm, supernatant basis |
| Model brine wastewater, 7 ppm | — | supernatant below detection limit | capture |
| Reuse, second cycle | — | 79.4% | dose not recorded; cause under investigation |

These are **capture** figures derived from the drop in supernatant absorbance. They do not include desorption and collection. **Recovery on real wastewater has not yet been measured.** Our business plan uses a recovery rate placeholder of 92.9%, which we flag wherever it drives a number.

### 3.3 Known gaps (disclosed at application)

1. **Polymer loss of about 30% per cycle** (up to 50%) in the current liquid-liquid separation, because the dilute phase retains dissolved polymer; this bears directly on consumables economics. A copolymer that precipitates as a solid (gravity settling instead of centrifugation) has been made; its loss rate is not yet measured.
2. **No continuous-flow data.** The lab protocol uses static settling and centrifugation; a producer has named continuous flow its heaviest requirement. A continuous-flow unit is the first engineering task.
3. **Iodine speciation.** Discharge may carry residual reducing agent, leaving iodide rather than iodine; pre-oxidation requirements are being defined.
4. **High salinity.** Phase-separation temperature shifts with salt (38 °C in water, 35 °C in brine). Behavior above 100,000 mg/L TDS, typical of US produced water, is untested.

### 4.2 Phase 2 — North America (conditional)

US iodine comes largely from oil-and-gas produced water in Oklahoma's Anadarko Basin, where Iofina operates eight plants (each 10,000–50,000 bbl/day) and has announced a Permian plant for late 2026. Back-calculating from disclosed output, we estimate these plants take water at **59–108 ppm**. Two customer groups follow:

- **Downstream of existing plants.** If plant recovery is 70–90%, residual streams run 6–33 ppm, comparable to or richer than Japan's tails.
- **Streams that never reach a plant.** Water-midstream and saltwater-disposal (SWD) operators aggregate produced water (one operator alone reports 60 disposal facilities and 1.2 million bbl/day of capacity). Below the concentration at which iodine value equals disposal cost (**51–106 ppm** at USD 74.27/kg and disposal costs of USD 0.60–1.25/bbl), no one builds a dedicated plant. A low-CAPEX retrofit owned by the water operator is the only form that works there.

One Japanese design site (about 13,000 bbl/day) sits at the lower end of a US plant intake, so the unit transfers without redesign. Two open questions decide Phase 2 and are what we want to test through GSC: **do low-concentration streams exist at meaningful volume at aggregation points**, and **does the polymer work above 100,000 mg/L TDS**. If either answer is no, Phase 2 stops. Iodine is not on the US critical minerals list; we do not build the case on subsidies.

## 5. Business model and unit economics

Three revenue layers, all keyed to a unit the customer owns and operates:

| Layer | Price (Sep 2026) | Nature |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1. Unit sale | JPY 100M per unit (about USD 0.63M) | one-time; customer CAPEX |
| 2. Consumables and maintenance | JPY 10M per year per unit | recurring polymer supply |
| 3. Process fee on recovered iodine | JPY 2,000 per kg-I₂ | recurring, volume-linked |

**Customer P&L per site, per year (design case):** additional iodine sales +JPY 50.9M; consumables −JPY 10.0M; process fee −JPY 8.6M; on-site power −JPY 0.6M; **net additional gross profit +JPY 31.6M**; simple payback **3.2 years** (about 2.2 years with Japanese investment tax incentives). All of these scale with the unverified recovery rate.

**Why asset-light is a consequence, not a preference.** Below the break-even concentration, iodine returns only part of the disposal cost, so a vertically integrated plant cannot pay for itself; the only design that works is a low-cost retrofit on existing assets, paid for by the asset owner and monetized through consumables and a volume fee.

**An unfavorable number we disclose.** Per kilogram of annual capacity our unit costs about USD 146 against about USD 44 for Iofina's newest plant, roughly 3.3×, because we process much thinner water. The gap narrows if North American tails are 20–30 ppm (3–4× output per unit) and is offset, in the customer's view, by the payback above.

**Company revenue (illustrative, FY2030, 5 sites):** about JPY 293M across the three layers. Cost of goods is being rebuilt after the September price revision, so we do not quote operating profit.

## 6. Competition and intellectual property

| Alternative | Position | Our reading |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| Blow-out and ion exchange (incumbent) | Economical at higher concentrations; used by all Japanese producers | Neither side has published data at 7–12 ppm. We do not claim the band is uncontested. |
| Iofina IOsorb (USA) | Vertically integrated, 8 plants, 59–108 ppm feed (estimate) | We sit downstream, not in competition for the same water. Risk: Iofina moves to lower concentrations. |
| WO2025258648A1 (a Japanese university with a domestic producer) | Adsorbent for ≥10 ppm; desorption with reducing agent | Closest patent. Ours desorbs by temperature swing; regeneration data for both are limited. |
| Reverse osmosis | Rejection collapses at low feed (about 10% below 15 ppm in one study) | Not viable in our band. |
| Do nothing | Discharge or dispose | The real alternative. We add revenue rather than replacing disposal. |

**IP.** A foundational patent application covering the polymer family is filed and published in Japan (Doshisha University, sole applicant; inventor: the PI). Our patent attorney has confirmed that a design outside the foundational claims is feasible; its structure is withheld until the **first application patent** (joint filing, Doshisha University and Kato; invention committee approval Aug 7, 2026) is filed in **October 2026**. Information is managed in three layers: public (function, targets, customer value), demonstration-required (operating conditions, analytics, connection specs) and core (formulation, manufacturing, control logic, cartridge internals). University-led public R&D results stay with the university under an exclusive option; company-led R&D is contracted so new IP is solely owned.
